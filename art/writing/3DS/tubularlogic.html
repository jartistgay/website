1.
Janis lies on their stomach, gazing down through the window at the world beyond, seeing only blackness. Occasionally, the rotation of the great machine will show them the light of a single star before it drifts out of view again. Rolling over, they look up at the belly of the machine: much the same. Mostly darkness, a few blinking lights.
Footsteps sound in the corridor. Barely lit, they can see the contour of a face peeking in. “Janis.” The voice they recognise as Inés, their shift leader.
“Yeah?”
“We need you.”
People are always asking after Janis. They can never get a moment of rest. They shift, first getting to their knees, then rising slowly. If they stand too fast, their head will hit the ceiling. As it is, they have to stoop down. It seems the machine wasn’t built for people of their height. They follow Inés through the doorway, careful not to snag their robe on the exposed guts of the machine. It’s not until the corridors start to widen that they can stand up straight.
“What is on your mind?” Asks Inés.
They sigh inwardly. “Does it always have to be something?”
Inés leads a snaking path through the corridors. “I am doing you a favour, Jan.”
What kind of favour, they almost ask, biting their tongue. “I’m just wondering why the Mother decided to give us a view of the world beyond.”
“Sacred sites.” Her voice is clipped, matter-of-fact. “We need places to meditate, to commune with the machine. The Mother is still out there, somewhere, and the windows are the closest we can get to her.”
Janis turns the idea around in their mind. They wonder why there’s no images of the Mother. Wouldn’t that be easier? No, it wouldn’t. Photos would fade with time, and the Mother is eternal. Not undying, but still ageless. They realise with a start that Inés isn’t leading them towards the peripherals of the machine, but instead towards the places where all the people are. The mess hall, the council room. “Um, what am I fixing?”
They can only stare at the skin of the back of Inés’s head, hair shorn to please the machine. Her silence leaves itchy daggers of sweat on their back, between their shoulder blades. How hard can it be to answer such a simple question?
“Nothing to fix right now,” she says.
Her response is terse, which means… What exactly? She could be tired. Or angry. Or any number of things. They nervously rub their fingers on the coarse fabric of their robe, rolling the hem of their sleeve. They pass by a dorm lined with beds; every person in the room stares at Janis as they pass. “Inés?” They ask, trying to keep their voice steady. “If I’m not fixing something, why do you need me?”
She doesn’t answer, doesn’t say a single word for the remaining walk. They pass through sparse rooms, rooms that normally have more people. They try not to notice, but every pair of eyes seems to be locked on them.
Once they reach the door to the council room, Inés touches the handle reluctantly. They see her take a deep breath before she turns it.
Inside, there’s barely any space to move. The entire place is packed with bodies; people sitting at the table, people standing, crammed against walls. It’s not even a tenth of the population of the machine, but they still feel as if every person they’ve ever known is here. Conversation is hushed, but extent. At the far end, atop the dais and the throne, sits the Librarian. Janis slips inside, trying their best not to touch anyone, to blend into the crowd, but even here, the eyes of strangers follow them.
Someone jostles them, pushing them towards the front of the room, and before they know it, the crowd of people has become a tide, a shifting current carrying them forwards, all the way to the front.
They’re unsure if they should kneel before the Librarian. Her face is wizened, her gnarled hands resting atop a small wooden box with a key sticking out of the lock. Real wood, probably. Her eyes are locked on Janis. After a time, they decide that kneeling is appropriate, and as they do, the voices of the assembled begin to die down.
“Leave us.”
Janis looks out of the corner of their eye to see people obeying. They want to follow, but they can at least read the room well enough to know that they are probably included in ‘us’.
Once the council room has emptied of people, the Librarian offers the box to Janis. “Take it,” she says.
They obey, hands trembling. Looking up for approval, they begin to turn the key. The Librarian doesn’t stop them, so they open it, retrieving the worn booklet inside. Real paper. “Your honour?”
She inclines her head towards them. “It’s yours now.”
“What is it?”
“The Mother left it to us.” She points down with a single finger. “On the cover is everything that keeps us alive.”
Janis studies the cover. It seems like a simple diagram, a flow chart, with all the arrows flowing in a circle. Every part is labelled with words such as ‘condensation’ ‘waste management’, ‘Sabatier system’, and ‘urine recovery’.
“They tell me I will die soon. I tell them I haven’t yet. Nevertheless, when I pass into the arms of the Mother, someone will need to take up my duties. I chose you.”
Janis looks up at the Librarian and the deep lines age has drawn into her face, the white of her close-cropped hair. “Why me?” They whisper.
She answers simply: “Because you’re the best we have.”
“That can’t be.” They almost drop the book on the floor. “They’re always telling me how I fail to live up to their expectations. The hair, the cloak, everything. What makes me fit to be the next Librarian?”


2.
“Librarian?”
Her voice is weary. “Yes, Janis?”
“Where does the food actually come from?” They lift a bag of something nondescript from the cart, examining it in the dim light. “We put the cart in the machine and, what? The machine produces the food for us? Every day?”
The Librarian looks at them. “The same place our water comes from. Our own waste is transformed by the modules. That’s how the cycle works. That’s what I’ve been trying to teach you.”
“But the food doesn’t come from all around the machine, or the modules that make up the machine. Do you remember a few years ago, when three modules broke all at once, and people died, because there wasn’t enough air? We’ve never fixed a module that makes food.”
“I remember.” She walks slowly, and Janis makes sure to slow enough that they walk side-by-side. “It’s always been my thought that the food modules are too complex for us to fix. Maybe the Mother is the one who maintains them.”
Janis sets the bag back in the cart. “I wish the Mother told us more about the rest of the world.”
“She did.” The Librarian coughs. “Space. It’s deadly. You saw what happened when the machine was breached.”
“I did. But the Mother can survive in a vacuum. Why can’t we?”
The Librarian laughs. “I knew you would be a handful. I just didn’t know it would be this bad.”
Janis’ voice is small. “I’m just trying to understand.”
“I know, I know.” She sighs deeply. “There are many things the Mother didn’t tell us. Has anyone ever told you that you’d rather not know something?”
“Yes, and they were always wrong. I’d rather be certain in my anguish than uncertain in it.”
“Maybe so.” She puts a hand on the cart, and Janis stops pushing while the Librarian catches her breath. “A long time ago,” she says. “Someone told me I’d rather not know why the food they made for me tasted so different.” She laughs. “Then I caught them pissin’ in it.”


3.
“Where does electricity come from?” Janis asks. They’re waist deep in a module, an oxygen scrubber, hoping it doesn’t muffle their voice too much.
“Stars.”
They jolt, thumping their head against the insides. “Ow. Electricity comes from stars?”
“Yep. There’s a star that’s very close to here. The center of the machine needs to be pointed towards it at all times, to gather as much starlight as possible. That’s why there’s no windows with a view of it.”
“So that’s why all the windows are at the far ends of the machine… near the flat parts of the cylinder?”
“Yep.”
Janis mulls this over as they finish up their repairs. When they finally extricate themself from the module, they wipe off their bare face and arms, putting their robe back on. “Why can’t I tell people about electricity?”
The Librarian’s face tightens. “They don’t need to know.”
“They don’t need to, but what if they want to? No. They’re not even allowed to want to know.” They sigh. “What’s the point? We spend forever tending to the machine, and then what?”
“The Mother welcomes us into her arms.”
Janis sighs, packing away their tools. “At the end of every line of questioning is the Mother.”
“She made us, and she will unmake us, when the machine has served its purpose.”
Janis raises an eyebrow at her. “And when will that be? What is the purpose of the machine? Of us?”
“Who knows? Now take that robe off.”
“I won’t, sorry.”
“Won’t cut your hair, won’t take off your robes.”
“You were the one who picked me. Deal with it.”
“Every module of the machine must have you in it.” She grabs at their head, running her fingers through their hair. A cluster of strands comes free. “Look at it. Unseemly.”


4.
Janis’ blood runs cold when they hear the screams. “Stars above,” they breathe.
The Librarian reaches out a hand, lifting the baby from the food cart. She rocks it gently. “This happens, occasionally. We take it, we raise it as our own. The child will never know it was not one of us.”
“The machine produces children, too?”
“Indeed it does.” The screaming dies down. “Your name will be Ximena,” she tells it.
“But…” Janis is breathless. “How? Why?”
She doesn’t answer, focusing on the child.
“Your honour, I can’t just stand here not knowing.”
Her voice is quiet, so as to not wake the baby. “They’re the Mother’s, of course. Why do you think we call her that?”


5.
“Librarian?” One of the engineers looks up at Janis, on the throne. “My condolences for your loss.”
They thank the woman, though they wish they didn’t have to. They just imagine themself, holed away at the outer edges of the machine, looking down at the stars. They like the stars.
They dream of what life would be like outside the machine. Blasphemous, perhaps, but it comforts them. The warm embrace of the Mother. What must her life be like? Floating out among the stars, completely alone. Lonely. Just like Janis.
The thought is reassuring, but only briefly. No, they remember. The souls of the dead are there to keep the Mother company. There truly is nobody like Janis, not anywhere.
Someone else comes up to kneel before them, offering more kind words. They accept them as gracefully as possible, knowing that the Librarian never managed to scrub that awkwardness out of them. The old Librarian.

8.
“Are you mad at me?”
“No,” the voice breathes. “No, I’m not.” More reassuring herself than Janis.
“I’m sorry.”
“I am too. I wish you could see the world beyond. The grass is green. The sky is blue.”
“That would be too much, I think.” They try to comfort the voice. “I already don’t like the lights here. Ground that is green? All the time? A blue sky? Where are the modules?”
The voice laughs. “I don't know what modules are. And it's not blue all the time, not when it’s dark. When it’s dark, everything is black and calm and quiet.”
“Forever?”
“No. Only as long as you sleep.”
“I think I would sleep when everything is bright,” they concede. “I wish things were darker here.”
The voice sighs. “What if you brought all of your people here? So you don’t have to take care of them?”
Janis’ voice is small. “Then who would maintain the machine?”
“I don’t know, Janis.”
Janis is quiet, but they make a leap, throw a rope, a tether, hoping to bind them to the voice on the other side of the ceiling. “You know my name,” they say, as quietly as they can while still having the sound transfer. “What’s yours?”
“Sofía Nayeli Andrea.”
They think for a minute. “That’s a long name.”
“Is that so?”
“You have three names. I knew someone like that once.”
“With three names?”
“Three people. In their head. Do you have three people?”
“No. Sofía is my name. Nayeli and Andrea are the names of my mothers.”
“I see. There’s so many people out there, that some people have the same name? And you need your parents’ names to keep track?”
“Essentially.”
“The old Librarian told me that we used to allow two people to have the same name, and we’d just call them ‘Number One’ and ‘Number Two’. But people didn’t like that, so we stopped. Your parents are named Nayeli and Andrea?”
“Yep.”
“What are they like?”
“They’re wonderful. You’d like them a lot, I think. Andrea has so many books. You could spend a whole lifetime just sitting there, reading.” Sofía says with milk in her voice. “I wish I could get some books to you… What are your parents like?”
“I never knew them.”
She is silent for a moment. “I’m sorry to hear that.” Her voice is full of sorrow.
“Why?”
“I love my mothers. I couldn’t imagine being without them.”
“No, they’re with me. I just don’t know who they are.”
“What?” Out of all their customs, this seems to be the one she is most flummoxed by.
“Well, we do it for a lot of reasons. It’s easiest to teach and raise the children if we do it all communally. That way, no child is neglected.”
“I suppose so.”
“Also, sometimes the Mother grants us new children. So it’s best if the new children are able to blend in with the rest. That way, nobody treats them differently.”
“When was the last time you got a child? From the Mother?”
They have to think about it. The concept of time having numbers is still a little strange to them. They knew about seconds and minutes and days, but not months, or seasons, or years. “Maybe four years ago.”
“The Mother…” She seems like she wants to say something, but she doesn’t. She only hums, gentle at first, then breaking out into a tune.
Sofía. Janis is glad to know her name.


9.
“Sofía?”
The voice doesn’t reply.
“Sofía? Sofía Nayeli Andrea?”
It’s their usual time, but Sofía neglected to show. Janis kicks at the wall. Damn this machine! They kick and kick until they think they broke something in their foot, and then kick some more. But they can’t make it through, can’t go out to where Sofía was.


10.
Janis lies on their stomach, gazing down the window at the world beyond, seeing only blackness. Occasionally, the rotation of the great machine will show them a single light before it drifts out of view again.
They don’t know how long they’ve been like this. The hunger gnaws at their insides, but they enjoy it. Occasionally, someone will call them for help. They will fix the module in silence. And they will return here, to watch the stars.
They lost their robe at some point. Submitted to having their hair removed. None of it matters to them anymore.


11.
“Hello?” A voice calls from the slot where food comes from. Janis freezes. It’s not Sofía. They can tell, probably. It’s been so long now that they don’t remember what her voice sounded like. But they’re sure they’d recognise it, if they heard it. And this person is angry.
A knocking resounds through the metal. “Mother?” Knocking again. “Mother, that's my wheat, you know! That’s mine! I know you need to eat, too, but…” The voice has emotion in it. “My family… they could’ve eaten that..!”
The voice dissolves into tears. “You bastard… Thief…”
It’s only when the sobbing is well and truly gone that Janis takes the cart of food and wheels it back to the mess.

13.
Janis looks down at their child. Nobody knows who the parents of any children are, save the Librarian, who must name them. They did their duty. And now they have to watch as their own child grows up without their parents to guide them.
They think of Sofía and Nayeli and Andrea. Even after all this time, they still daydream about Andrea’s library, Nayeli’s cooking. With only the Mother for guidance, they fear that children grow… stunted? Isolated?
Like them?
The others seem to fit in just fine. It’s only Janis who’s the outcast. They take a deep breath and send a prayer to the Mother that their child doesn’t end up like them.

Her face is impassive as always, but she seems reluctant to say whatever it is she has to say. But she shakes her head and musters the courage. “Is Regina ours?”
“Yes.”
“Okay.” She takes a deep breath. “That was easier than I thought it might be.”
Janis shrugs. “I thought you deserved to know, out of everyone. If you asked.”
Her eyes narrow. “If I asked.”
“Yeah, I wouldn't tell you if you didn't want to know.” In that moment, they seem a little sad. “Sometimes stones are best left unturned.”
“I - Janis.” They don't know how they know, but they can tell she's chastising them. “You are not supposed to keep track!”
“Like I could forget! I have to name them all! Look, it's not like I played favorites in choosing her.”
“Of course,” Inés scoffs, “she is our child. Of course she would be the best. That is not why I am mad at you.”
They frown. “Then why are you mad at me?”
“I am mad because I would have liked to have known.”
“You know now.”
“That's not the same. You have been able to raise her and I have been left out in the cold.”
“But sometimes you don't like when I tell you things, like when I told you about that mole on your-”
“Stop.”
They sigh. “How was I supposed to know this is one of the things that you wanted to know about? And besides, like you said, I'm not supposed to remember.”
Inés shakes her head. “I guess I am blaming you for the way that you are. And I am blaming myself for not possessing the courage to ask sooner.” Her eyes widen in a way that Janis has never seen before.
“Janis?” Asks Regina. “I'd like you to meet Camila.”
Camila waves shyly. “Hello.”


24.
The four of them emerge from the hole in the metal into complete darkness. It's not until Camila lights a lantern that they can see.
“What even is that,” asks Janis, as they reach out a finger.
“Don't,” says Camila, “it's hot.”
Regina examines their surroundings. “I think it's more important to ask: where are we?”
“I've told you about the cave,” says Camila defensively.
“Yes, but being here is so… disorienting. This whole place is massive.” She spreads her arms out wide, spinning in a circle. “Only the mess even comes close!”
Camila takes her hand. “Wait until you've seen outside.”


25.
Sofía slaps them. “Janis, you fool.”
They look up at her. “What did I do to earn that?”
“She doesn't know she's your daughter?!?”
“What-” Regina starts, but Inés cuts in.
“I have been wanting to slap them for years.”
“It's irrelevant,” says Janis. “I practically raised her. Why is it important that she's my daughter?”
Sofía looks over to Inés. “You're the other parent?” She nods. “How do you deal with this one?”
“I do not, outside a strictly professional capacity. And I recommend you follow my example. They will win.”
Sofía rolls her eyes. “Of course. Janis, she needs to know you love her.”
“She knows,” Janis says simply as they look to their daughter.
Regina nods, looking at each of her parents in turn. “I wouldn't choose anyone else to be my parents.”
Sofía's expression tightens. “What I meant was that she needs to know you chose her.”
“I didn't,” they say. “Nobody chooses to have children; everyone just does their civic duty when the time comes. I am the Librarian. It is my duty. And-” they cut Sofía off as she starts to talk again, “-and I did choose her to be the next in line. Not because she's my daughter, but because she's the best there is.”
“I told you what would happen,” mutters Inés.
“Maybe we should..?” Says Teresa with a hand on her lover's shoulder.
“No,” Sofía says. “She never knew Inés as her mother.”
Regina hangs her head slightly.
“I have come to terms with this,” says Inés. “But it is clear that the girl has not. An undue cruelty.”
Regina's mouth is screwed up. She purposefully doesn’t look at her mother. “It would have been nice to know why you were so…”
Inés nods. “I am to blame for that.”
Sofía takes a deep breath gripping the table. “Sorry. I just-” she swallows and moves to the centre of the room, the dim lamp now properly illuminating her. Janis notes her mostly grey hair, once black, is poofy, cut between the chin and ear.
“Her mothers were very important to her,” says Teresa with an apologetic smile towards the other parents. “You must understand.”
Janis inclines their head. “It's no issue at all.”
“Moving on,” says Sofía, a little louder than is necessary. “We need to discuss.”
“What is there to discuss?” Asks Inés, looking down as her daughter takes her hand.
“Our children are going to want to see each other. No, even if we agree that our people should never have direct contact, we can't rightfully stop them from crossing over. What if someone down there recognizes that Camila isn't one of you?”
Janis puts a hand to their chin as Inés says, “And we would not want to stop them, either.”
“And why would we not show everybody?” Cuts in Regina furiously.
Sofía purses her lips. “It isn't that that simple.”
“It isn't that simple,” echoes Camila, squeezing Regina's other hand. “People fear what they don't understand.”


26.
“Carmen Regina Camila!” Her mother calls. “I'm going up! Tell your mother that- hey! That's my book!” She rushes over to Carmen and gently pries the manual from her little fingers. “That's not a toy, baby.” She tucks it into her bag with one hand as she kneels, using the other to ruffle her daughter's hair. They get old so fast… Carmen's curly hair is well past her shoulders now.
“Can I come with you?”
Regina can't resist those wide eyes. So curious. “Where's your mother?”
“Right here!”
Regina laughs. “Your other mother, where's Camila?”
Carmen considers. “Definitely somewhere,” she says solemnly.
“You've been spending too much time down here with Inés. Camila!” She calls.
“Yeah?”
“Where are you?”
“The bindery!”
Regina makes her way through the corridors of the machine, leading her daughter by the hand. The new bindery isn't well stocked, but it's nice and cozy. “Carmen and I are going topside to test the receiver. Oh, hey Janis.”
Carmen breaks free of her hand, rushing over to her grandparent. “Librarian!” She calls, although she can't quite get the consonants correct.
Janis pulls her into their lap. “Hey sweetie,” they say.
“Hey," says Camila, “Stay on task. I need more folios.”
“Sorry,” says Janis, apologizing to both her and their granddaughter as they lower her back to the floor. “Another time,” they promise, and Carmen nods sagely.
“You're going up?” asks Camila.
“Yep.” Regina once again takes her daughter's hand. “Still working on the tram, so we'll be walking.”
She nods as she punches holes with the awl. “You're going to find aliens this time?”
Regina grins. “Who knows? And where's Lucía, If she's not with you?”
“She's with Inés,” says Janis, waving her away dismissively. “Now go test my invention.”
“Your invention?” Regina scoffs. “The thing hardly worked when I-” Regina pouts when she realizes the others aren't paying attention. “Fine,” she says with faux-haughtiness, turning. “Let's go Carmen. We’re not wanted here.”
Carmen runs up ahead and then turns around, jogging backwards through the corridor. “Mom! Can I have a ride?”
Regina smiles and crouches down. “Of course baby. Here.” Carmen clambers onto her shoulders, and the two march off. “Just remember to duck when we go through the caves. I don't want you to bump your head.”

27.
It's Lucía with her this time as she sits in a field, the sun beating down on her. She tries not to let her sweat drip into the receiver.
“What does this do?”
“Hmm, imagine it as a big ear.” She nods forwards. “Take a few steps away from me.” Lucía obliges. “I’m going to keep talking, and you’re not going to be able to hear me that well. But you’re going to cup your hands around your ears, and suddenly you’re going to hear me a lot better. That’s because your hands are reflecting the sound back into your ears. You can come back now.”
“But I can hear you just fine from over here!” She calls.
Regina laughs. “Ok, ok. So this bit is like a hand around your ear, but for radio waves. So we can pick up incoming transmissions.”
“And the thing in the middle?”
“That’s the feed antenna, the part that actually receives the transmissions. So I guess it’s your ear, in this analogy.”
After a moment, Lucía gets bored and wanders closer. “And can I help you?”
“Soon, baby. Have you been keeping up on your lessons with Janis and Inés?”
“Yep!”
“Then soon. I’ll ask them how you’re doing, the next time I see them.”
Lucía nods and goes off to play in the field. Regina works for a time, although how long, she is unsure.
“Need help?” A shadow falls over her.
“Hmm, well,” she laughs, gesturing to the receiver. “Janis was no help at all.”
“I will be better,” Inés promises, groaning slightly as she lowers herself to her daughter's level. “I was going to entertain Lucía, but if this is where I am needed…”
“Here first,” she says. “I know we have to build a bigger dish, but it'll be a while before it's finished.”
“The blacksmith?” Regina nods. “Ah, the resources at your disposal. I always forget that there is all… this, up here.”
“At least you're better than Janis,” says Regina.
“No, no, they just…” she taps a screwdriver to her chin. “How to put it? They enjoy doing things the hard way, sometimes. I think that they fear that they will run out of things to do.”
“Fat chance.”
“Well, there was a time where that was a genuine concern. Before you and Camila broke down the barrier between our societies.”
Regina shrugs. “At the time, I just wanted to be with her.”
“And now?”
She squints up at the artificial sun, wiping her eyes clean of sweat. “Forwards, I guess.”
“I am not trying to dissuade you,” says Inés. “Oh, here, you wired the filters wrong. But, you know what they say about the great beast named Progress.”
“All those who chase it are eaten by their own desires,” she says with consternation. “It hasn't eaten me yet.”
“That is a tautology,” says Inés. “If it had eaten you, you would not be able to say that.”
Regina scoffs. “Go play with Lucía, she's probably lonely.”
“Indeed,” she says, brushing herself off as she stands. “And I am sorry to butt in more, but are you sure that a parabolic receiver is best for the job?”
“This is just prelim,” Regina grunts as she works. “I'll install better receivers on the outside once I've perfected them. And the parabolic is the only one strong enough to penetrate through the radiation shielding.”
Her mother smiles. “Good thinking.”


28.
“You know, mom says space is deadly.”
“Radiation.”
“Yes, I know radiation, and the vacuum too. But look,” she flips through a leather hand-bound notebook, a birthday gift from her grandparent. “See, we can build a big coat and pants with fabric that blocks radiation.”
“Lead?”
“No, I think lead's going to be annoying. But we'll figure out something.” She slowly thumbs through the pages, showing her sister sketches and designs. “The hardest part is carrying enough air with you.”
Her sister taps a finger against the paper. “What about using that?”
“The tether?”
“Yeah, just make it a big tube and leave it connected to the ship. Although,” she taps her chin, “it would be hard to make a port to connect it to the inside.”
“No no,” says her sister. “Keep going! I think you're on to something…”


29.
Janis folds paper, singing quietly to themselves. That's one of the things Sofía really loves about them. How to put it into words? It's not the fastidiousness, and it's not the musicality, but something about the combination thereof is so distinctly them.
“Hey.”
Janis doesn't look up, and they don't smile. “Hey.”
Maybe it's stupid, but the lack of overt affection they show is part of the charm. She knows, at least, that if it were almost anyone else they would have stopped singing. Pretended they never were in the first place. But she's one of the half-dozen around whom Janis feels comfortable singing. And for a while, she just stands there, listening to them sing a harmony to a melody she doesn't know.
When they finish the batch of folios they look up at her. “Is there anything you needed?”
She smiles. “So utilitarian.”
Janis shrugs and begins a new tune.
“But where does the singing come in?” She wonders aloud.
They frown at their work. “The utility of song?” She nods, and they say, “You might as well ask the utility of joy.” They shake their head. “Joy isn’t a tool. It's a goal.”
Sofía nods. That's another thing she likes about them: their clarity. They understood what they were about long ago. And Sofía, now almost 60, with grandkids entering their teens, is just figuring it out. “Do you ever wonder what it would be like? If we were…”
“Like Regina and Camila?” She nods. “On occasion. More idle fancy than anything.” They punch holes into paper with the awl. “No changing the past. Besides,” they meet Sofía's eye for the first time in the whole conversation. “Aren't you happy like this?”
Sofía considers. “I am, and yet…”
“You find your mind wandering.” They nod sympathetically. “Humanity is a long chain…” they say, running a length of twine over a block of beeswax, “a long chain of-” they shrug. “Not progress. No, socially, politically, we move back and forth. Technologically, too. Just look at our civilizations. No, we are a long chain of history. Tautological, perhaps: our past is history.” They thread the twine into a curved needle. “Cause and effect.” They stack the folios, sewing them together. “If we couldn't do it, then the next generation would. And if they couldn't… So on and so forth.” They sigh, looking at their handiwork.
“I think fate is like that: dictated by human nature. Curiosity, first and foremost.” Their face, without warning, becomes burdened with a sadness she cannot comprehend. “We are in-ex-tric-a-ble.” They enunciate each syllable clearly. They bury their face in their hands. “In-ex-or-a-ble.”


30.
“It's my invention! Why can't I test it?!”
“Your invention?!” cries her sister. “Not true! If it wasn't for-”
Regina cuts them off, pinching between her brows. “Stop it. Neither of you are going out there.”
Lucía crosses her arms, tapping a foot proudly. “Only we know how to use it. Anybody attempting to operate it would be learning on the fly, and that would be unsafe, now wouldn't it?”
Janis laughs, gnarled hands wrapped around their book. “She has you there!”
Regina looks to Camila. “Are you going to back me up here?”
She shrugs. “We did dangerous things when we were that age.”
“Not that dangerous! Not ‘vacuum of space’ dangerous!”
Inés puts a hand on her daughter's shoulder. “You have to let them take risks sometimes.”
“Are you all out of it? They're fifteen!” When nobody rebuts her, she looks at her daughters. “Fine.” Camila takes her hand. “Please be careful,” she says quietly.


31.
“How are you doing up there? Over.”
“Good. Installation is going smoothly…”
“You didn't say over. Over.”
“That's because I'm still thinking. I had something else to say… A lot of the solar panels are broken… Over.”
“Elabourate. Over.”
“Maybe not broken, necessarily. What did Carmen get hit by that one time? When she punctured her suit and had to patch it? Over.”
“Micrometeoroids? Over.”
“Anyway, there's a handful that are broken. Probably from micrometeoroids, but some just look worn. From what, I couldn't say. Over.”
“Radiation, maybe? Over.”
“Could be. Do you think we could try to make new ones? Over.”
“You'd have to harvest a few; probably bring them back here. Over.”
“I'd love to, but, well. Put it on a work order. It's just that Carmen is so busy with the baby. Could we get Inés to help? Over.”
“Mmm. She's… her hands have gotten bad enough that… We could ask. Over.”
“We'll ask. Over.”


32.
Carmen and Lucía press the glass of their helmets together.
“It's so weird being out there with another person.”
“Yeah,” Carmen laughs. “So freeing, but also grounding. I love it.”
“Here, can we hold hands?”
“Let's see… a little difficult with the gloves.”
“Yeah.”
“I love all the changes you've made out here. It's been years since I've been on a spacewalk.”
“All to improve the lives of… everyone down there. It seems so tiny from the outside. Even though we're not even that far away.”
"Yeah.”
They float in silence for a while, just enjoying the view.
“Do you feel that?”
“Yeah… what is it?”
“I can't tell…”
They both look around, searching for the source of the disturbance.
Janis’ strained voice comes in over the radio. “Picking up transmissions. Are you two good out there? Over”
“We felt something weird, but we haven't tried to talk to you,” says Lucía, “Wait. Transmissions, plural?” But before she can say ‘over’, she sees something that takes her breath away.


33.
Janis hobbles through the sterile corridors of a foreign spaceship. “I knew it!” They say, laughing, leaning on their daughter for support. “I knew there was more out there. All my life…”
The archivist, who is nearly as old as they are, smiles. “This is what I do it for, you know. Connecting people. The Gods left a lot of their civilisations disconnected from the wider galaxy, which I think is a mighty shame.”
“The Mother left us disconnected from each other, until Regina here broke down the wall between us.”
The archivist’s eyebrows lift. “That's got to be one interesting story. I’d love to take a record of it for the archive.”
Janis nods. “My granddaughters invented the spacesuits we used. It's a shame they can’t see all this.”
“We'll dock here for a few weeks, at least; they'll get their turn. For now, would you like to see the archive?”
Their eyes alight. “Would I?”

Alma:
You haaate referencing characters by name in the beginning of a section, huh?

J:
Eep! I guess so. I wish I could pin down what about this is so interesting to me. I guess on a fundamental level, I love nothing more than keeping the audience guessing. You should always be on the back foot, in my opinion. It really frustrates me when a story seems to truck along on autopilot for a while, or at least, assumes that's the state of mind I'm going into it with. I don't want to be on autopilot, I want to think! And, consequently, when someone reads my stories, I want them to think.
Another element of it, I think, is that I want every scene to feel like it's just a scene the reader is wandering in on. Stuff was happening before this, and things will happen after this, but we don't need to see all of it. The reader can assume that X or Y happened, even when it's not directly shown, almost like the implied motion between comic panels. You asked me about [28], and how much of science do they have. The Periodic Table? Are they doing everything from scratch? And that's really up for the reader to assume. What do you think makes the most sense? What bits from earlier in the story corroborate your theory? I leave these gaps because, well, that's not necessarily what the story's about. But also, I think it's very engaging, as a reader, for the writer to leave so much on the table for you to draw your own conclusions on.
