1.
Janis lies on their stomach, gazing down through the window at the world beyond, seeing only blackness. Occasionally, the rotation of the great machine will show them the light of a single star before it drifts out of view again. Rolling over, they look up at the belly of the machine: much the same. Mostly darkness, a few blinking lights.
Footsteps sound in the corridor. Barely lit, they can see the contour of a face peeking in. “Janis.” The voice they recognise as Inés, their shift leader.
“Yeah?”
“We need you.” People are always asking after Janis. They can never get a moment of rest.
They shift, first getting to their knees, then rising slowly. If they stand too fast, their head will hit the ceiling. As it is, they have to stoop down. It seems the machine wasn’t built for people of their height. They follow Inés through the doorway, careful not to snag their robe on any of the exposed guts of the machine. It’s not until the corridors start to widen that they can stand up straight.
“What is on your mind?” Asks Inés.
They sigh inwardly. “Does it always have to be something?”
Inés leads a snaking path through the corridors. “I am doing you a favour, Jan.”
What kind of favour, they almost ask, biting their tongue. “I’m just wondering why the Mother decided to give us a view of the world beyond.”
“Sacred sites.” Her voice is clipped, matter-of-fact. “We need places to meditate, to commune with the machine. The Mother is still out there, somewhere, and the windows are the closest we can get to her.”
Janis turns the idea around in their mind. They wonder why there’s no images of the Mother. Wouldn’t that be easier? No, it wouldn’t. Photos would fade with time, and the Mother is eternal. Not undying, but still ageless. They realise with a start that Inés isn’t leading them towards the peripherals of the machine, but instead towards the places where all the people are. The mess hall, the council room. “Um, what am I fixing?”
They can only stare at the skin of the back of Inés’s head, hair shorn to please the machine. Her silence leaves itchy daggers of sweat on their back, between their shoulder blades. How hard can it be to answer such a simple question?
“Nothing to fix right now,” she says.
Her response is terse, which means… What exactly? She could be tired. Or angry. Or any number of things. They nervously rub their fingers on the coarse fabric of their robe, rolling the hem of their sleeve.
They pass by a dorm lined with beds; every person in the room stares at Janis as they pass. “Inés?” They ask, trying to keep their voice steady. “If I’m not fixing something, why do you need me?”
She doesn’t answer, doesn’t say a single word for the remaining walk. They pass through sparse rooms, rooms that normally have more people. They try not to notice, but every pair of eyes seems to be locked on them.
Once they reach the door to the council room, Inés touches the handle reluctantly. They see her take a deep breath before turning it.
Inside, there’s barely any space to move. The entire place is packed with people; people sitting at the table, people standing, crammed against walls. It’s not even a tenth of the population of the machine, but they still feel as if every person they’ve ever known is here. Conversation is hushed, but extent. At the far end, atop the dais and the throne, sits the Librarian. Janis slips inside, trying their best not to touch anyone, to blend into the crowd, but even here the eyes of strangers follow them.
Someone jostles them, pushing them towards the front of the room, and before they know it, the crowd of people has become a tide, a shifting current carrying them forwards, all the way to the front.
They’re unsure if they should kneel before the Librarian. Her face is wizened, her gnarled hands resting atop a small wooden box with a key sticking out of the lock. Real wood, probably. Her eyes are locked on Janis. After a time, they decide that kneeling is appropriate, and as they do, the voices of the assembled begin to die down.
“Leave us.”
Janis looks out of the corner of their eye to see people obeying. They want to follow, but they can at least read the room well enough to know that they are probably included in ‘us’.
Once the council room has emptied of people, the Librarian offers the box to Janis. “Take it,” she says.
They obey, hands trembling. Looking up for approval, they begin to turn the key. The Librarian doesn’t stop them, so they open it, retrieving the worn booklet inside. Real paper. “Your honour?”
She inclines her head towards them. “It’s yours now.”
“What is it?”
“The Mother left it to us.” She points down with a single finger. “On the cover is everything that keeps us alive.”
Janis studies the cover. It seems like a simple diagram, a flow chart, with all the arrows flowing in a circle. Every part is labelled with words such as ‘condensation’ ‘waste management’, ‘Sabatier system’, and ‘urine recovery’.
“They tell me I will die soon. I tell them I haven’t yet. Nevertheless, when I pass into the arms of the Mother, someone will need to take up my duties. I chose you.”
Janis looks up at the Librarian and the deep lines age has drawn into her face, the white of her close-cropped hair. “Why me?” They whisper.
She answers simply: “Because you’re the best we have.”
“That can’t be.” They almost drop the book on the floor. “They’re always telling me how I fail to live up to their expectations. The hair, the cloak, everything. What makes me fit to be the next Librarian?”


2.
“Librarian?”
Her voice is weary. “Yes, Janis?”
“Where does the food actually come from?” They lift a bag of something nondescript from the cart, examining it in the dim light. “We put the cart in the machine and, what? The machine produces the food for us? Every day?”
The Librarian looks at them. “The same place our water comes from. Our own waste is transformed by the modules. That’s how the cycle works. That’s what I’ve been trying to teach you.”
“But the food doesn’t come from all around the machine, or the modules that make up the machine. Do you remember a few years ago, when three modules broke all at once, and people died, because there wasn’t enough air? We’ve never fixed a module that makes food.”
“I remember.” She walks slowly, and Janis makes sure to slow enough that they walk side-by-side. “It’s always been my thought that the food modules are too complex for us to fix. Maybe the Mother is the one who maintains them.”
Janis sets the bag back in the cart. “I wish the Mother told us more about the rest of the world.”
“She did.” The Librarian coughs. “Space. It’s deadly. You saw what happened when the machine was breached.”
“I did. But the Mother can survive in a vacuum. Why can’t we?”
The Librarian laughs. “I knew you would be a handful. I just didn’t know it would be this bad.”
Janis’ voice is small. “I’m just trying to understand.”
“I know, I know.” She sighs deeply. “There are many things the Mother didn’t tell us. Has anyone ever told you that you’d rather not know something?”
“Yes, and they were always wrong. I’d rather be certain in my anguish than uncertain in it.”
“Maybe so.” She puts a hand on the cart, and Janis stops pushing while the Librarian catches her breath. “A long time ago,” she says. “Someone told me I’d rather not know why the food they made for me tasted so different.” She laughs. “Then I caught them pissin’ in it.”


3.
“Where does electricity come from?” Janis asks. They’re waist deep in a module, an oxygen scrubber, hoping it doesn’t muffle their voice too much.
“Stars.”
They jolt, thumping their head against the insides. “Ow. Electricity comes from stars?”
“Yep. There’s a star that’s very close to here. The center of the machine needs to be pointed towards it at all times, to gather as much starlight as possible. That’s why there’s no windows with a view of the star.”
“So that’s why all the windows are at the far ends of the machine… near the flat parts of the cylinder?”
“Yep.”
Janis mulls this over as they finish up their repairs. When they finally extricate themself from the module, they wipe off their bare face and arms, putting their robe back on. “Why can’t I tell people about electricity?”
The Librarian’s face tightens. “They don’t need to know.”
“They don’t need to, but what if they want to? No. They’re not even allowed to want to know.” They sigh. “What’s the point? We spend forever tending to the machine, and then what?”
“The Mother welcomes us into her arms.”
Janis sighs, packing away their tools. “At the end of every line of questioning is the Mother.”
“She made us, and she will unmake us, when the machine has served its purpose.”
Janis raises an eyebrow at her. “And when will that be? What is the purpose of the machine? Of us?”
“Who knows? Now take that robe off.”
“I won’t, sorry.”
“Won’t cut your hair, won’t take off your robes.”
“You were the one who picked me. Deal with it.”
“Every module of the machine must have you in it.” She grabs at their head, running her fingers through their hair. A cluster of strands comes free. “Look at it. Unseemly.”


4.
Janis’ blood runs cold when they hear the screams. “Stars above,” they breathe.
The Librarian reaches out a hand, lifting the baby from the food cart. She rocks it gently. “This happens, occasionally. We take it, we raise it as our own. The child will never know it was not one of us.”
“The machine produces children, too?”
“Indeed it does.” The screaming dies down. “Your name will be Ximena,” she tells it.
“But…” Janis is breathless. “How? Why?”
She doesn’t answer, focusing on the child.
“Your honour, I can’t just stand here not knowing.”
Her voice is quiet, so as to not wake the baby. “They’re the Mother’s, of course. Why do you think we call her that?”


5.
“Librarian?” One of the engineers looks up at Janis, on the throne. “My condolences for your loss.”
They thank the woman, though they wish they didn’t have to. They just imagine themself, holed away at the outer edges of the machine, looking down at the stars. They like the stars.
They dream of what life would be like outside the machine. Blasphemous, perhaps, but it comforts them. The warm embrace of the Mother. What must her life be like? Floating out among the stars, completely alone. Lonely. Just like Janis.
The thought is reassuring, but only briefly. No, they remember. The souls of the dead are there to keep the Mother company. There truly is nobody like Janis, not anywhere.
Someone else comes up to kneel before them, offering more kind words. They accept them as gracefully as possible, knowing that the Librarian never managed to scrub that awkwardness out of them. The old Librarian.


6.
“The Librarian?” Inquires the ceiling. “Why do they call you that?”
“I keep the book,” they say with pride.
“The book?” The ceiling can't help but laugh. “Just one?”
Their cheeks redden. “It’s the only one there is.”
“That isn’t true,” says the ceiling. “I have a book right here.”
Janis frowns. “Read it to me.”
“Eve’s gaze was fixed on the dismembered body splattered across the train platform as it was briefly illuminated in flashes of pure red. Her nose was filled with blood.”
“What's a train?”
“I dunno.”


7.
“Join me,” says the voice from above.
“I can’t,” they say. “I would be killing my people. They rely on me to maintain the machine. I can’t take an apprentice for a long time yet. And that’s if you’re even telling the truth. If there really is something out there.”
The voice is silent.


8.
“Are you mad at me?”
“No,” the voice breathes. “No, I’m not.” More reassuring herself than Janis.
“I’m sorry.”
“I am too. I wish you could see the world beyond. The grass is green. The sky is blue.”
“That would be too much, I think.” They try to comfort the voice. “I already don’t like the lights here. Ground that is green? All the time? A blue sky?”
The voice laughs. “Not when it’s dark. When it’s dark everything is black and calm and quiet.”
“Forever?”
“No. Only as long as you sleep.”
“I think I would sleep when everything is bright,” they concede. “I wish things were darker here.”
The voice sighs. “What if you brought all of your people here? So you don’t have to take care of them?”
Janis’ voice is small. “Then who would maintain the machine?”
“I don’t know, Janis.”
Janis is quiet, but they make a leap, throw a rope, a tether, hoping to bind them to the voice on the other side of the ceiling. “You know my name,” they say, as quietly as they can while still having the sound transfer. “What’s yours?”
“Sofía Nayeli Andrea.”
They think for a minute. “That’s a long name.”
“Is that so?”
“You have three names. I knew someone like that once.”
“With three names?”
“Three people. In their head. Do you have three people?”
“No. Sofía is my name. Nayeli and Andrea are the names of my mothers.”
“I see. There’s so many people out there, that some people have the same name? And you need your parents’ names to keep track?”
“Essentially.”
“The old Librarian told me that we used to allow two people to have the same name, and we’d just call them ‘Number One’ and ‘Number Two’. But people didn’t like that, so we stopped. Your parents are named Nayeli and Andrea?”
“Yep.”
“What are they like?”
“They’re wonderful. You’d like them a lot, I think. Andrea has so many books. You could spend a whole lifetime just sitting there, reading.” Sofía says with milk in her voice. “I wish I could get some books to you… What are your parents like?”
“I never knew them.”
She is silent for a moment. “I’m sorry to hear that.” Her voice is full of sorrow.
“Why?”
“I love my mothers. I couldn’t imagine being without them.”
“No, they’re with me. I just don’t know who they are.”
“What?” Out of all their customs, this seems to be the one she is most flummoxed by.
“Well, we do it for a lot of reasons. It’s easiest to teach and raise the children if we do it all communally. That way, no child is neglected.”
“I suppose so.”
“Also, sometimes the Mother grants us new children. So it’s best if the new children are able to blend in with the rest. That way, nobody treats them differently.”
“When was the last time you got a child? From the Mother?”
They have to think about it. The concept of time having numbers is still a little strange to them. They knew about seconds and minutes and days, but not months, or seasons, or years. “Maybe four years ago.”
“The Mother…” She seems like she wants to say something, but she doesn’t. She only hums, gentle at first, then breaking out into a tune.
Sofía. Janis is glad to know her name.

Oh, that's absolutely something I love in a story, characters who are exactly who they seem. Not that it can't be fun, getting to know someone's backstory or having an unreliable narrator, but there's a very autistic joy in someone being so relentlessly straightforwards, considering that most human being are not.

Also, it's a fun subversion that you just never see. One of my favourite little writing tools is making the reader think that there might be a conflict, and then making them realise, actually, that conflict would have been dumb! Isn't this so much better?
