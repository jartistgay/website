TAPE ONE
Track One: On Goat Rock (In Winter)
Making promises I know I can't keep, as a safeguard for my thoughts.
The founding monument looms above the crowd: a sphere, cracked down one side, with the first Dual and Caster rendered in stone atop it. The rest of your class mills about. Laughing, talking, jostling with sharp elbows. You’re sitting at the edge, chin in your hands, so you can look at all of it at once.
You’ve been in the center of the city before, for class trips, or to meet friends. It’s not like it’s easy to miss; the statue is almost twenty feet tall. The buildings crawl around the bowl in which you sit. Voices echo against the walls, reaching your ears as garbled reflections.
You keep your hands at your sides, tunneled in your sleeves. Your fingers are always cold. You look around for Iris, hoping to ask him to put your hand in his. You spot him as he breaks from the crowd and settles beside you. You reach over, and even though he gives you a look, he obliges. His hands are bigger than yours.
As voices continue to rise, you look at the monument again. There’s a little magic to it, you think, something that makes it shimmer in your peripheral vision like a heat mirage. The founders, the first bonded, could still be out there somewhere. Godhood arrests the body’s decay, but does not grant invulnerability. Life is never eternal, death only delayed. Perhaps they’re already dead: their bones resting on some backwater planet with nobody to find them.
Louth, the Headmaster, makes her way to the front. She bears the hallmarks of a Caster: navy robes, two left hands.
She doesn’t call for silence, but her presence is enough. The people settle down slowly, their conversations dying as they turn to look at her. Even after all the talk has ended, she doesn’t say anything, just letting the moment steep.
She smiles. “Long ago, before this chunk of land was lifted to its rightful place among the clouds, we fought for independence.”
The words have no real impact. You don’t know if they’re transfixed out of fear or attention. The silence enjoys itself.
“The land was earthbound, a ruin, a crater. Dust had been made of nations. From that collapse rose two souls: a magician and a protector. They devised the method for bonding. It was the first shared spellcraft.” She gestures to the statue, to the Gods who founded your civilisation. “They were not engineered for compatibility, like you are. They were strangers. They chose each other. But more importantly, they chose discipline.”
You look to Iris. The fingers of her other hand curl in: tight, looser, then tight again.
“This is what we honour,” she continues. “Not talent, nor fate, but the bond. The clarity it brings. Caster and Dual are alone unfinished. Together, they exceed their sum, uplifting our home, our people.”
“When you step here with me, you will begin your long journey towards completion. When you are finished, you will be capable defenders of Anima. Welcome to the academy, Newlings.”
No applause follows. You had forgotten to breathe, rendering your head light and uneven. When you stand, it all rushes back to your head, and you lean on Iris for support.
Louth beckons, and you all move as one body towards the monument. A bell chimes, low and deliberate, and the statue slowly begins to descend.
You look down at your hand, still in his, and then back up. The Dual’s face is serenity incarnate. The Caster’s smile almost reaches the eyes, their curly hair like a halo about them.
You think, unbidden: they didn’t want to be here either.
The statue grinds, stone against stone, slowly lowering down. Your palm sweats. As it disappears, a new platform slides into place: a simple unadorned slab. Louth steps onto it, beckoning the rest of you.
“I’m sure I don’t have to tell you, because I see everyone has dressed appropriately. Well,” she smothers a chuckle with her hand as the platform slowly descends. “almost everyone.” There’s some laughs and shoves from nearer to her; a kid you think you recognise wears only a skirt and sleeveless shirt. “Apologies, but you were warned. We’ll be taking a hike through the snow, and it’ll be long enough that you’ll feel the cold.” She raises an eyebrow at the student. “Perhaps if you run ahead of us, it would warm you up?” Something in her delivery communicates that it’s a joke, although you can’t tell what. Her voice is just as flat and dry as before.
The platform continues to lower; the light from above recedes enough that eventually everything is shrouded in darkness. You try not to acknowledge that you’re leaving behind only city you’ve ever known.
“Hey,” you say. “You know how Anima is shaped like a funnel?”
“Yeah?”
“We’re in the middle right now.”
“Oh.”
You don’t know what you’re trying to accomplish in saying that. You can’t see her face to try to interpret her expression. You tighten your hand in hers.
As the top edge of the platform crosses an invisible boundary, white light streams over the lip of the disc, providing an aerial view of the academy grounds. Snow blankets the entire landscape; only small slivers of trees peek through the monotonous white. You think you can spot a set of buildings in the distance, but it’s difficult to tell.
The cold also strikes you. It’s freezing here. You knew it; you wore your thickest coat and pants and a nice pair of boots. But it’s never this cold on Anima, only snowing once every few years in a light dusting, so unlike this place that has only ever known frost and ice. You don’t know how long the Academy’s existed. Somewhere upwards of 1500 years, if you had to guess.
Your view shifts as you descend further, less and less of the landscape becoming visible. Then you’re level with the treetops, and it’s only a moment before you settle against the ground.
“Alright!” Louth calls out. “Everybody off!”
You clear the platform, huddling together, and she kicks the side, sending it slowly reeling back up into the sky. “By the way,” she adds, marching to the head of the group, “don’t try calling the platform back down. You can’t, and it’s a waste of your time to try. Follow me.” She sets out into the forest, and the rest of you follow.
You trudge through the snow in silence. The heat is visible as it escapes your body, condensing the water vapor in front of your mouth. Your fellow classmates chatter away quietly, but you and Iris content yourselves with contact. But a voice calls his name up ahead. His hand squeezes as he looks at you. You nod, and your palm grows cold as he jogs to catch up.
Not like you could follow them, anyway. Casters aren’t made for pace. You’re too frail, too brittle in the joints. Sometimes you wonder if you’re intentionally designed to be weak, or if it’s a inextricably tied with the ability to cast. You don’t know which would be worse. You walk alone. The snow is thick, and your boots match. They do little to help. Every step drags you downward, slower and slower, as though the ground resists being walked on, unwelcoming of the newcomers.
Behind the pack, a student in Caster blues falls in with your pace, all lazy stride and fluid gait. He glances back: calculating escape. Then he sees you, and brightens like you’re a coincidence he’s pleased by, as he hums a little tune you only catch the end of.
His coat is near black draped over regulation blues. His trousers are tight, denim, not school issue. His hair is long, improperly so, past the shoulder. Duals must shear theirs for armor’s sake. Casters are merely encouraged. He seems older in the way that some children just age unevenly. Yet his face is unplaceable. Stylised. Less a face than an impression, instead something remembered.
He snaps his fingers once, pointing. “Kos, right?”
You watch his hands. Long. Elegant. Frustratingly perfect.
“Uh. Have we met?”
He smiles, wide and immediate, conspiratorial. “Think we took a PPA class together. You remember me? Got scolded for, uh - creative applications.”
You would remember. “No, I don’t. Sorry.”
His eyes alight. “Oh! Well then, I have a trick to teach you.” He snaps with a thumb and forefinger. A flame, no larger than a candle’s, flickers into being. “Portable lighter. Convenient, yeah?”
You’re horrified. “That’s not allowed. No spells until instruction begins. It’s dangerous.”
He flushes. Real embarrassment. It almost makes him...
“Then keep it quiet, huh? Our secret. I’ll teach you.”
You offer your casting hand unconsciously. His fingers guide yours into place. “Leftie. Huh. Alright. You need to cycle power through the digits: counterclockwise, for you. Gotta use your thumb and forefinger. Have you noticed most people snap with the middle? Anyway, you build the charge, snap to break it. That’s the release.”
You mimic. The flame appears. Small, but alive.
He looks genuinely pleased. “Well done. Took me two days, my first time.”
Your finger starts to burn from proximity to the flame. You shake it out in panic. “Saints Ascended, that’s painful!”
He shrugs and turns to go, accelerating. You reach out.
“Wait! But that’s - !”
He glances back with a frown. You’ve earned his disapproval. “It’s not a spell. No Conspecs, no language component, no structure, just energy work. Not illegal; if it’s a safeguard for your thoughts."
You simply stare at him, brows knitted loosely.
“We’ll see each other in class,” he says with a halfhearted gesture of respect and departure, and he’s gone. The snow resumes its slow sabotage of your ankles.


⇶ ⇶ ⇶

You do not speak during orientation. No one does, really. The procession is a solemn trudge through the institution’s arteries: corridors, empty stone hallways, and the faint gleam of arcana on almost every wall, if you look hard enough.
You and Iris are diverted down a narrow passage. Your dorm is Level Three-East. The corridor hums with sterile quiet. The doorplate is etched in wonderfully serifed letters: KOS & IRIS. For a moment, you imagine a different name there, LOVEDAY.
Inside is a single bed, a narrow desk, and a thick glass window with an enchanted seal. The lights give off the hum of hospitals and detention centers. The air smells of chemical cleanliness. You find it comforting. It reminds you of the time you spent in Holding a few years back. Your relationship with Iris had deteriorated soon after Warren left, and you weren’t right in the head. You don’t know what happened to her during that time.
Iris unpacks with precision. Uniforms folded, items arranged. Her dark hair, freshly shorn, catches the room’s cold glow. You sit on the bed. You do not unpack. Her mouth is tight and her motions are mechanical, as though she’s done this many times before.
You speak without meaning to.
“He should’ve been here.”
She doesn’t respond.
“Warren would’ve mocked that speech. Especially the bit about falling. Remember how he used to climb everything? He would’ve called Louth a tyrant.”
Her hands slam flat on the desk.
She turns. Her face is unreadable, but her eyes are not.
“Stop.”
You stop.
“He’s not here,” she says, jabbing a finger your direction. “Don’t make it worse by pretending he is.”
You nod slowly. You don’t apologize. You’re not permitted to apologise to her anymore.
She leaves. The door shuts softly. She’s gone to the training floor, probably. Somewhere to hit something.
Snow begins to fall outside. You lie back, staring at the ceiling. Too low. You hear students above, laughing and joking, as though any of this is normal.
You think of Warren. Not his face this time, but his voice rustling from the branches of a pomegranate tree in an orchard near the wall, his hand reaching down towards you.
“Don’t look down. Looking down makes you fall.”
You close your eyes and try to dream of the ground.

J:
Oh, that's absolutely something I love in a story, characters who are exactly who they seem. Not that it can't be fun, getting to know someone's traumatic backstory or having an unreliable narrator, but there's a very autistic joy in someone being so relentlessly straightforwards, considering that most human beings are decidedly not that.
Also, it's a fun subversion that you just never see. One of my favourite little writing tools is making the reader think that there might be a conflict, and then having them realise that, actually, that conflict would have been dumb! Isn't this so much better? The conflict that they imagined provides the same catharsis when the reveal happens, and I don't have to write something I didn't want to write!
