he woke up and he knew that the world had changed, but not how.
“Twenty-five.” The voice of the doctor rang out clear like the daytime, followed by a horrified murmur from the aide in the typical blue of the operating room.
he laid in an infirmary bed, white blanket, white gown; illumination not the typical off-kilter green hue of a CFL or tubelight that one would expect to find in a medical office, but rather a dim and gentle gold which imparted upon him the idea of a late evening. he had no window here, but he knew that it couldn’t be night; the clinic only ran until five.
“What?” he tried to mumble, but it didn’t come out right; he felt the effect of the knock-out drug lingering.
“Hmm?” The doctor looked over and down. “Ah. How are you feeling?”
“Mmgh,” he managed, all he could get out of an uncooperative mouth.
“You’ll feel fine in a minute. Relax.”
“Twenny fhive?”
The doctor laughed, brilliant white teeth peeking out. “Nothing to worry over. We were talking about an unrelated matter.”
he knew in the heart that it couldn’t be true. “What happened.”
The doctor clicked a tongue. “Well, you know that we implanted in you a device.” He pinched a gloved thumb and forefinger, indicating it being no bigger than a dime. With the other hand, he rubbed the back of the neck. “I can’t tell you any more - an unfortunate part of the whole deal with your employer. We want you to notice if anything- hm?” He turned to the aide. “Ah. Well, the health check came through. You’re cleared to leave whenever you’re comfortable.”
“You can’t tell me what you did to me?”
The voice of the doctor happened to be gentle: “Louie. Let me reiterate: part of the contract - you can’t know what happened. Tell the lab if you think you notice any change. You remember that much?”
“Louie?”
“Your name?”
It couldn’t be correct. “Not Louie. Not my name.”
The doctor, blank faced, thumbed through a clipboard, holding out one piece of paper. Initially it blurred, but indeed the name at the top read ‘Louie’. “Would it be ok if I marked that down for a change?”
“Um. Yeah.”
He pulled out a pen and made a note. “Any other query?”
“No.” he put a hand on the table to lever upright. “I’m kinda dizzy.”
“You’ll be dizzy for another minute or two. You’re waking up from an operation, after all.” He checked the clock that hung above the curtain dividing the room from the hallway. “Ah, I need to attend to another patient. Have a good day, Louie, and at the end of the week, tell me what you’ve noticed. We’ll take it from there.”
And the doctor left.
he didn’t know what to do except remain there and wait for the feeling to fade. But it didn’t fade, not when he talked to the clerk at the counter, not when he marked a date and time one week from now in the calendar app, not when he wrote down their emergency number, not when he left the clinic, not on the walk in the parking lot, not when he got into the car.
When he put the key into the ignition, he felt a lance of panic - could he drive after the operation? Nothing in the material implied he couldn’t, but he didn’t want to get into an accident. He glanced at the emergency number he had written down only a moment ago. Did he want to bother them from their own parking lot? No, he would call the public number. Better careful than regretful.
he dialed the office. “Hello, Louie Monaghan here. I have a query.” The bitter quality of the name that did not belong to him hadn’t abated.
“Louie?” he had a note of uncertainty, there until it left. “Oh, Louie. I remember now. How can I help you?”
The man at the front had forgotten him? That quickly? “I’m feeling off, that lingering feeling of being wrong I talked about. I’m not dizzy anymore, but I would like to double check. Can I drive?”
“Drive?”
he ran a finger over the wound and the polymer thread at the back of the neck where they put in the device that hooked up to the… what part of the body? he didn’t remember. “I mean, I don’t know what they did to me, that thing in my head.” he muffled a nerve-induced laugh. he didn’t want to appear loony. “I think that would be a normal thing to worry about.”
“All too normal,” The clerk agreed, calming, gentle. “You’re perfectly ok to drive. The operation won’t interfere with anything that important; we’ve had a very long time to perfect it, after all. You might tell me that what you’ve gone though today will change the remainder of your life… well, the doctor would joke about it being an average Monday.” The laugh a practiced one. “I remember, way back when the neural implant got invented. That early period had a fair portion of - well. It could be rough. Do you? Remember?”
“No, I don’t.” he recalled that the clerk didn’t look much older than he did, but he decided to drop it. he took a deep breath, in and out, attempting to calm down. “Ok. Thank you for your help. Have a good day, now.”
“Have a good day.” he hung up.
With reluctance, he turned the key. The engine roared to life, vibrating the car gently. he recalled a memory, unbidden, of driving with a friend when they were young. The memory brought a melancholy that he hadn’t expected. What had happened to that friend? he had the feeling that a thing had happened, that they weren’t in contact any more, but he couldn’t recall exactly what. he couldn’t even picture the face. Couldn’t think of the name. What letter did it begin with?
“No,” he murmured.
Even though he remained off balance, he put the car into drive and pulled out of the parking lot. he tuned the radio and a track came on, one he recognized. he hummed along, tapping the wheel in time with the beat. Then the vocal part came in, and he noticed that it had been changed; probably a remix of one kind or another. What rankled even further: every other part of the track remained like he remembered. It made the brain itch, prickling like acupuncture. How dare the new, interloping band ruin a work of art that he loved?
he turned off the radio, now wanting to drive home in quiet contemplation. Yet no matter what mental track he followed, nothing felt right. The day beat down upon him, bright light interfering with perception, leaving a void here or there. It didn’t prevent him from driving, but he felt on edge the entire way. he didn’t remember anything like it before; would he call the office? Inform them? No, he had already called them in a panic. he would write it down and they would know at the end of the week.
he pulled into the demarcated portion of the parking bay, grabbed the handbag from next to him, and locked the car. he trampled the lawn, ignoring the notice that reprimanded him for that exact action; he needed to be in the apartment that he called home, in comfortable apparel, right now, and no later.
he took the elevator one floor up, key clutched tight in hand. A chill overcame him when he turned the key in the well. he couldn’t know how he knew, but he knew before he opened the door that a human being waited in the kitchen.
And indeed, at the counter, pretty and round faced, with long hair and makeup on the mouth, he perched on the nice wooden chair that Louie had pulled out of the Goodwill. he loved that chair, had removed the original waxy lacquer, had rebuilt a leg, a rail, more; for a whole month he did very little except repair it.
he loved that chair.
“You need to get off my chair, immediately.” he jammed a finger at the intruder. The comedy occurred to him, that the chair happened to be more important than a criminal breaking into the apartment.
he jumped up, twitchy, like Louie had pointed a gun at him. “Louie!”
he frowned. How did he know the name, the one that couldn’t feel right, no matter how often he turned it over? he didn’t recognize the intruder at all. Or did he? “Leave right now or I call the police.”
“Louie.” he peered at him, gaze narrowed, looking for… for a thing he couldn’t place. “Are you ok?”
“Ok?” he laughed. “Why would you think that I would be? Would you be ok if a criminal broke into your home?” He reached into the handbag, palming the mace he kept there. “Evidently not - you thought that you could break in, expected me to, what? Welcome you?”
“No,” The intruder breathed out. “Not at all. You’re talking weird, Louie. Can you… call the doctor? Explain to them?”
he again felt a prickling at the back of the brain. “The doctor?”
“You went in for an operation. For an experiment? And now you don’t recognize me.” The countenance of the intruder had taken on a new, childlike pain. Near crying. How did he know about that?
Inhabiting a body made him want to vomit, it made him ill. he had a… what did they call it? Like paparazzi, but for people who weren’t well known. he couldn’t be a celebrity; he worked a boring office job, in accounting.
“I’m begging you,” The paparazzi pled, crying. “Talk to the doctor.”
“Fine.” he kept one hand on the mace, but with the other, he redialed the office. “Louie here. Look.” he, too, now, had begun to cry. And when he looked at the intruder, their pain had changed to puzzlement. “I hate to call again, but I’m in my apartment right now. I don’t know how to talk about it… A human broke into my home. They know too much about me. I want to kick them out, but they told me to call you. I don’t know what to do.”
“ofia,” cried the intruder. “My name. ofia.”
“They claim to be named ‘Ofia’.”
The intruder overlapped with the one on the phone: “Not Ofia! ofia!”
The voice of the operator dug at him: calm to the point of farce. “I get it. Would you mind handing the phone to… the intruder?”
“Fine.” hand firmly on the mace, with great trepidation, he approached the intruder - Ofia. What an uncommon name. he had never heard it before.
What happened between Ofia and the phone operator, he didn’t know, for they were both quiet in their talk. he could only watch from the doorway.
Ofia let out a moan. “I have nowhere to go.” Their voice had been laced with a powerful pain he didn’t think could be contained in one human. After a moment, he held out the phone. “he told me to give it back to you.”
Louie accepted it with reluctance. “Hello?”
“Hello, and I apologize in advance, but the intruder… well. They’re part of everything.”
“What?” with incredulity, he looked to Ofia. “That can’t be true.”
“I apologize again, but he won’t hurt you. You’ve lived there with him for a long time. You’re unable to remember, though. Put your faith in me when I guarantee that you will remain unharmed.”
he couldn’t breathe very well. What the hell had he gotten into? “Are you - how do you know?”
he heard paper being touched. “Right here in your file. You live with ofia Monaghan.”
“Ofia… Monaghan? Who? My brother?”
The line went quiet. “If you think that will help.”
“Who then, if not my brother? My father?” That couldn’t be it. Ofia looked to be of nearly identical age, thirty five, maybe.
“Not your father, Louie.”
he could tell he had been wearing thin the patience of the operator. he wanted to get more clarification, to cube the circle once and for all, but… the experiment. he didn’t want to ruin it, to give up, to quit; he needed the money. he exhaled. “Fine. Thank you for your help.”
“Thank you, Louie. Don’t forget to call if you have any more… ahem.”
he got the implication, the innuendo meant to paint over what had happened. “Have a good day.”
“You too.”
he hung up. Pointed again at the intruder. “We are not going to make it work.”
“We aren’t?” he looked oddly at Louie. “If only you heard me when I-”
“Ofia.” he interrupted.
“ofia.. Not ‘Ofia’”
“I know your name. Ofia. You keep correcting me with the exact name I’m calling you. Nothing coherent. I can’t comprehend anything right now.” he couldn’t bear to look at the world, blocking it all out with a hand. “I can’t even communicate how much difficulty I have communicating.”
Face covered, he could only hear Ofia, and he heard a footfall, one quick, then three more. They began and ended abruptly. he could picture the face of the unknown man very clearly, each pre-programmed pattern imagined perfectly. And when he looked up, what he imagined hung there in front of him. he didn’t want to believe the operator, that he knew an unfamiliar man better than he knew the face he gazed at in the mirror, but he had no other choice.
Pity. The face only had one emotion.
“You’re talking in a manner I find very… odd.” he watched the mouth when Ofia talked - it didn’t match the auditory qualia he experienced. Ofia reached out a hand, and he wanted nothing more than to bat it away, but he held back. “That could be why.” The finger halted before it reached him.
he had forgotten the train of thought that took them here, and couldn’t do the addition required to comprehend what Ofia tried to imply. “I’m going to cook.”
Ofia only looked at him. Pity, pity, pity. He couldn’t bear it.
he put a pan on the burner, throwing oil into it before he even decided what he wanted to make. Pulled a cookbook from the niche on the counter, thumbing through it apathetically. The page warped, moving. he couldn’t pin anything down, like that time in college when a roommate had given him DMT. Each paragraph, each word that made it up, they moved. Every time he zeroed in on a word, it congealed into a coherent pattern, but looking at it from afar, the whole thing looked underwater. With great patience, he read each word of the title one by one, copying them onto a notepad.
When he had written it down, he held each paper at the end of an extended arm. The title in the cookbook had not changed; the eye rejected it. But the notebook, to great puzzlement, didn’t. Right there, in neat handwriting:
“Lamb and Bean tew”
tew? he didn’t know anything about ‘tew’. Had never heard of a meal like that. he looked at the picture. The picture portrayed the titular lamb and bean in a pot; a weirdly liquid meal. What were they called? Liquid food? Like a chowder, a food floating in a broth. A chill ran through him. Whatever the doctor had done, what the neural implant had been doing, it changed… well, everything about reality. Nothing felt right anymore.
More than anything, he wanted to go to bed.
he turned off the heat, running the pan under the faucet. But the more he looked around, the more he noticed that he couldn’t name half of what he kept in the kitchen. he poured detergent into the… what? The little blue thing that had been riddled through, like he had taken a needle and jabbed it over and over and over again. The whole time he cleaned the pan he looked at it, unable to decipher the name.
When he had placed the pan to dry on the rack, he held the cleaning object, hunched over the counter. How could he not recall the name of an object that he touched every day of the week for longer than he could remember?
he exhaled, long and drawn out. he wanted to cry, but he couldn’t.
When he turned around, Ofia had not moved. Louie again tried to reach for a word but failed. Ofia watched him, watched with intent, doing nothing but watching him. he felt a tear leak out in impotent anger, hand reaching for a word that he had known once, but not now.
“You’re watching me.”
Ofia didn’t reply.
“I want it to be over.”
he again did not move, did not even react.
“I want you to no longer perform the action of watching.” he felt the inability to form the word, the multiplicity of word, that he could not create with the mouth and tongue. “You will look at me no longer.”
Ofia finally complied. Without ceremony, he turned, opened the front door, and left.
Louie looked at the entrance in an identical manner to how Ofia had looked at him trying to cook.
he walked to the bedroom, flopped down into a bed too large for only one body. he barely bothered to change, even though he had wanted that more than the world, not a quarter of an hour ago.
In the hole that had been left, he rotated what he knew he had been trying to communicate.
Do not look at me any more.
You cannot gaze upon me in the way you are doing right now.
You will end the action of looking at me.
You will conclude…
You will terminate…
You will halt…
But no matter how hard he tried, he could not hold it in the mind.
he drifted off, unable to comprehend what had been forcibly removed from him.
But humans don’t understand the brain, either.
In her bed, Louise slept, and she had dreams.
Dreams where she knew that the world had changed, but not how.
