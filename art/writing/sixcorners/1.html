chapter 1
Eve’s gaze was fixed on the dismembered body splattered across the train platform as it was briefly illuminated in flashes of pure red. Her nose was filled with blood.
The RaC kicked her in the back of the knee, and she hit the ground face-first. The rough skin of her cheekbone felt nice against the concrete, she thought, as he put her wrists in fetters. “Your rights under the Cascadian Constitution are as such:”
She was unconcerned with his voice or the information it was attempting to communicate. She was focused on the smell of iron; there had to be something beneath it. And, in the strange way of memory, when she found it, she was transported to another time and place.
Before she became a doctor, before the ice breaks forced her out of her childhood home, she laid on the couch with her sister, smelling smoke. That had been when the house burned down; when life had sent both of them careening off in different directions, where they had once been sympatico.
Her mind was so deep in the memory that she felt she might have been unconscious during the trip to the courthouse and when she was brought before the judge.
“Eve Goodhart?” He asked. “Is that your name?”
“Yes, sir.”
“And is this you, in the footage?” All attention in the room turned to an old-fashioned projection screen. The blindfolded court tech pulled the publicam footage from the murder; everyone was treated to a wonderful show. A woman who looked like Eve swung a knife at the person Eve had seen in pieces on the platform. The footage sped up as the woman knelt over the body, taking it apart in less than sixty seconds, by the timestamp. Her cuts were quick and efficient, and Eve’s surgeon’s mind was pleased with the sight.
Then, for no reason at all, the killer stood, her frontside soaked in blood, and walked off screen, disappearing into one of the subway tunnels.
“Is that all?” The judge asked. “Can we see where she went?”
Unused to speech, the tech's voice cracked slightly. “No, your honour. There are no cameras on the tracks.”
The judge frowned as the footage continued, showing Eve stepping onto the platform, her shock at the discovery, and moments later, her arrest.
“What was your question?” Eve’s voice, like the tech’s, was strained.
“I asked if it was you in the footage.”
She cocked an eyebrow. “And how would I go from the tunnel to the staircase on the floor above in less than ten seconds.”
“How indeed.” The judge eyed the RaC, who shifted nervously. “You can’t deny the resemblance, though.”
“My sister,” she said.
The judge nodded to the tech, who, after a moment’s search, shook their head. He tapped the wooden desk with his fingers. “You don’t have a sister.”
“I do. My parents were natalists. We were twins. Fraternal. They had us at home, and only registered one of their children.”
“And how does she go about with no record?”
“I don’t know, your honour.” Tired of the commotion, Eve’s shoulders slumped in defeat. “I thought I had seen her. We haven’t talked in a long time. So I chased her down into the station.”
“And that’s where you happened upon this… scene.”
“Yes sir.”
“Just out of curiosity, Ms. Goodhart, where are you from? Your parents were natalists? Past tense?”
“I’m from Port Costa, sir. Village along the Carquinez. Our parents died, and the house burned down. The ice got bad enough that we had to flee for the bay. They separated us in foster care.”
“And that was the last time you saw her?”
Eve groaned internally. She really didn’t want to be answering these questions. And she knew that at this point, the judge was only mining her for data. “No. When we went to college, we were close again. But only for maybe two years. Then I had residency and she had whatever it is she did.”
“What did she get her degree in?”
“Visual arts. And I’m not sure she ever actually got the degree. If that’s all, sir?”
“Where do you live, Ms. Goodhart?”
Doctor. “Jingletown, sir.”
“What’s a doctor doing in Jingletown?”
She grit her teeth. “If there’s any more questions that are relevant to the charges..?”
He smiled. “You’re free to go. I’m dropping the charges against you.”
The cuffs around her wrists opened automatically with a soft click - courtesy of the tech. As she left the courtroom, she could hear the judge admonishing the RaC. “She didn’t even have a speck of blood on her..."
It was cold outside; almost three in the morning, although the rain was lighter than was typical.


A knock at the door woke me. I was surprised to hear it; nobody knocks on doors in general, and there was nobody to knock at my door in specific. I groaned when I remembered that the cam was out. That was why someone was knocking, and meant I couldn’t check who it was. No matter, it was just a solicitor. I rolled over and attempted to resume my respite, but the knocking persisted until I was forced to hunt for a shirt somewhere on the floor. The one I procured was black with yellow word art delineating the neighbourhoods of San Francisco.
I opened the door to the RaC that arrested me last night and slammed it closed as quickly as I was able. But he wedged a boot in the frame. “Ms. Goodhart.”
“Fuck off, man.”
He slid a laminated card through the gap, at eye level. I inspected it. “Detective Sha, ma’am.”
Detective? “The SFPD still exists?”
He sighed. “Yes it does, ma’am. If you could please let me in?”
I hesitated, but only for a second. If he was SFPD, he could get himself into my apartment pretty easily. Or make my life a living hell in any number of ways. “Just give me a moment to tidy up.”
“Of course.”
I shut the door and swept up the kitchen, throwing the dirty dishes in the sink. I let him in and gestured for him to sit at the little wooden table as I put a pot on. “Would you like something to drink, Detective?”
“No coffee for me, thanks. It messes with my ears. A glass of water, perhaps?”
